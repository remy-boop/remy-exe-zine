After shipping the Reviews Bot I needed a palette cleanser. Something built purely for the joy of doing it, and to satisfy my quirky, weird sense of humor. No stakeholder. No ticket. No KPI to report.

I also wanted to push further into integrating the Anthropic API into platforms beyond the ones I already knew. That's when I started thinking about *WhatsApp*. And if WhatsApp, why not horoscopes. And if horoscopes, why not make them *sharp*.

<PullQuote>
What if the stars knew about your product backlog?
</PullQuote>

The concept: you opt in with your zodiac sign, the bot asks how your day is going, and then it roasts you for your answer. Three rounds over a few days, like one of those old SMS subscription services you'd sign up for and half-forget about. Each round ends with a PM KPI, a fake but painfully plausible metric calibrated to whatever you just typed. After round three the bot says goodbye and goes quiet.

The goal wasn't a plain chatbot interaction where you prompt and it answers. It was a small recurring experience with a real arc. Something that got a little more accurate each time and knew when to stop.

<Panel id="p-fix" num="02" title="BOSS FIGHT">

I found I could use the Twilio sandbox to play around and build a real experience inside *WhatsApp* without needing a paid account or business approval. A sandbox number, a join keyword, and you're in. Good enough to ship something real.

The design challenge split into two fronts. Getting the tone right. And getting the mechanics right. Both took longer than expected.

<div class="section-divider">
  <div class="divider-line"></div>
  <span class="divider-label">▸ GETTING THE TONE RIGHT</span>
  <div class="divider-line"></div>
</div>

The question was how to make an AI horoscope feel funny and controlled instead of generic and weird. The first attempts read like a corporate chatbot introducing itself. Warm. Safe. Nobody was going to screenshot that.

<div class="fix-grid">
  <div class="fix-card before">
    <span class="fix-tag">✗ FIRST ATTEMPT</span>
    "Welcome! You registered as Scorpio. How's your day going?"
    <br/><br/>
    The sign meant nothing. Could have been sent by any chatbot ever built.
  </div>
  <div class="fix-card after">
    <span class="fix-tag">✓ WHAT LANDED</span>
    "You've already read everyone in the room, decided who to trust (no one)... How's your day going?"
    <br/><br/>
    One sentence. Sign-specific. Makes a Scorpio feel seen before they've typed a word.
  </div>
</div>

The key was to stop treating the opener as a greeting. The bot has already made a judgment. It's just telling you what it noticed. That same framing carried into the roast: not reacting to what you said, but confirming what it already suspected.

The opener is generated by Claude on every registration. Same sign, different line each time. The prompt gives Claude the sign's specific work dysfunction and tells it to write one sentence that sounds like it already knew you were coming. No hardcoded strings.

<div class="section-divider">
  <div class="divider-line"></div>
  <span class="divider-label">▸ BUILDING THE ROAST</span>
  <div class="divider-line"></div>
</div>

Early roast outputs felt like therapy sessions. Helpful. Reframing. Offering perspective. Completely wrong vibe. The prompt went through six iterations before the output started landing consistently.

The first fix was tone direction. Instead of asking Claude to "write a funny horoscope roast," the prompt described the character: a senior PM who has seen this pattern a thousand times and is not impressed. Dry. Deadpan. No warmth. No hedging. Just the verdict. The second fix was structure: give Claude the sign's specific work dysfunction AND the user's exact answer, and tell it to use one against the other. That combination is what makes it land.

After those iterations the roast bot finally started delivering genuinely funny, screenshottable experiences. The combination of sign dysfunction plus the user's own words used against them was the unlock.

<div class="section-divider">
  <div class="divider-line"></div>
  <span class="divider-label">▸ THE SESSION WINDOW PROBLEM</span>
  <div class="divider-line"></div>
</div>

WhatsApp has a platform rule enforced by Twilio: you can only send free-form messages if the user texted you first within the last 24 hours. That window opens on inbound, lasts 24 hours, then closes. The original plan was a daily 8am push. The bot reaches out, asks how your day is going. The window rule killed it. You can't initiate outside an active session.

So the product flipped to pull-based: the user texts <code>roast</code> to trigger the next round. What looked like a platform limitation became the core mechanic. The user controls the pace. That's actually a better product than an alarm that goes off whether you want it or not.

The nudge still works within the window. The user just texted to get the roast, which opens a fresh 24 hours. At hour 23, before the window closes, the bot sends a heads-up: "The stars have a new roast ready in 1 hour. Send roast to begin." The user texts <code>roast</code>, the window resets, and round 2 begins. The full arc looks like this:

<div class="section-divider">
  <div class="divider-line"></div>
  <span class="divider-label">▸ KEEPING THE NUDGE ALIVE</span>
  <div class="divider-line"></div>
</div>

The nudge had to be sent before the 24h session window closed, not after. Sending it at hour 23 meant the message was guaranteed to land within the active session the user opened when they got their roast. A simple DB queue: when a roast fires, insert a row with <code>send_at = now + 23 hours</code>. A cron queries pending nudges every 60 minutes, fires them via Twilio outbound, marks them sent. Server restarts don't matter because the schedule lives in the database, not in memory.

The 24h cooldown on <code>roast</code> requests also needed a message. If the user texts <code>roast</code> too soon after their last one, the bot replies: "the stars need 24 hours to recover from your last answer." Clean state machine, no silent failures.

<Gotcha tag="⚠ GOTCHA · TWIML IS NOT HTTP">
Twilio webhook replies require <code>Content-Type: text/xml</code> and a TwiML envelope: <code>&lt;Response&gt;&lt;Message&gt;...&lt;/Message&gt;&lt;/Response&gt;</code>. Regular JSON responses are silently ignored and Twilio falls back to its own default demo reply with no error, no warning. Outbound messages like the nudge use a completely different path: <code>twilio.messages.create()</code>, not TwiML. Two reply mechanisms on the same platform. One of them fails invisibly.
</Gotcha>

<Win tag="✓ WIN · SCHEDULE IN THE DB, NOT MEMORY">
Persisting the nudge queue in Postgres instead of memory meant the cron survived every server restart cleanly. A row with <code>send_at</code> and <code>sent = false</code> is a reliable job. A <code>setTimeout</code> in a process that can get killed at any point is not.
</Win>

When the prompt described the voice without examples, "dry, deadpan, like you've seen this sign a thousand times and you're not impressed," Claude generated sharper, more varied material every time. The direction is more useful than the sample.</p>

    <div class="xp-subhead">product thinking</div>
  </div>

        <p class="xp-learning-desc">The welcome message went through six iterations. Every version that felt off shared the same problem: too generic, too warm, or too on-the-nose. The right version sounds like the bot already knew you were coming. That is not a copywriting problem. It is a product question about what the user should feel in the first second of interaction.</p>

        <div class="xp-learning-title">GIBBERISH IS CONTENT</div>
        <p class="xp-learning-desc">When a user sends nonsense, that is not invalid input. It is a signal about their state. Someone who sends <em>asdkjhaslkd</em> is either testing the bot, avoiding the question, or having a day so bad they can't form words. All three are roastable. The bot treats the non-answer as data and responds accordingly.</p>
      </div>
    </li>
    <li class="xp-learning">
      <div class="xp-learning-body">
        <div class="xp-learning-title">DESIGN AROUND SESSION WINDOWS, NOT AGAINST THEM</div>
        <p class="xp-learning-desc">WhatsApp's session window rule meant cold daily pushes were impossible on sandbox. The user had to initiate each round by texting <code>roast</code>. But once they did, the window was open for 24 hours. Sending the nudge at hour 23 meant it landed within that window, re-engaged the user, and kept the arc alive without needing any special permissions. Understanding the constraint precisely is what made both the pull mechanic and the nudge possible at the same time.</p>

<section class="xp-section">
  <div class="xp-heading-row">
    <h3 class="xp-heading">▸ DEV SECRETS DISCOVERED</h3>
    <div class="xp-subhead">development learnings</div>
  </div>
  <ul class="xp-learnings">
    <li class="xp-learning">
      <div class="xp-learning-body">
        <div class="xp-learning-title">MESSAGING PLATFORMS HAVE TWO REPLY PATHS</div>
        <p class="xp-learning-desc">Most messaging APIs have a synchronous reply path (responding directly to an inbound webhook) and an asynchronous outbound path (initiating a message independently). They often have different formats, authentication, and constraints. Treating them as the same thing is a common source of silent failures.</p>
      </div>
    </li>
    <li class="xp-learning">
      <div class="xp-learning-body">
        <div class="xp-learning-title">SCHEDULED JOBS BELONG IN THE DATABASE</div>
        <p class="xp-learning-desc">In-memory timers die when the process dies. A row in a database with a <code>send_at</code> timestamp and a <code>sent</code> flag survives restarts, crashes, and redeployments. A lightweight polling loop that reads and processes pending rows is the foundation pattern behind every production job queue.</p>
      </div>
    </li>
    <li class="xp-learning">
      <div class="xp-learning-body">
        <div class="xp-learning-title">AN IDLE POLLER COSTS NOTHING</div>
        <p class="xp-learning-desc">A process that checks a queue every 60 minutes sounds expensive until you realize that finding zero rows costs essentially nothing. The query is the guard. When there is no work, there is no work done. This pattern scales naturally from a single user to thousands without changing the architecture.</p>
      </div>
    </li>
  </ul>
</section>
